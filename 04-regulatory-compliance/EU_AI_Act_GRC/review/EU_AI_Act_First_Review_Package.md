## 1. Purpose

This review package establishes the legal baseline, control architecture, recurring travel-agency case study, sample chapter structure, and visual-design rules for the English master edition.

The manual will not use decorative graphics. Every figure must explain either:

1. a process a person or organization must understand; or
2. a real concern an affected person may have.

## 3. Current regulatory baseline

The original AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its application is phased. Prohibited practices and AI-literacy duties began applying on 2 February 2025. Governance rules and obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models began applying on 2 August 2025. Most remaining provisions apply from 2 August 2026. Following the 2026 AI Omnibus amendments, specified Annex III high-risk uses apply from 2 December 2027, while high-risk systems embedded in regulated products apply from 2 August 2028.

Article 50 transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026. These include informing people when they directly interact with certain AI systems and requirements concerning AI-generated or manipulated content.

## 5. Control-writing pattern

Every substantive requirement will use this sequence:

**Requirement → Plain-language explanation → Travel-agency example → Control activity → Evidence → Audit test**

Where relevant, the section will also include:

- affected-person concern;
- human decision point;
- escalation trigger;
- override or correction route;
- accountability owner;
- retained evidence.

## 6. Core human-centred principle

AI may recommend, rank, predict, summarise, flag, or draft. It must not obscure who remains accountable.

For each AI use case, the organization must define:

- what the AI is allowed to do;
- what requires human review;
- who can approve, correct, reject, pause, or override the output;
- when escalation is mandatory;
- how affected people are informed;
- how a person may challenge or correct a consequential outcome;
- what records demonstrate that oversight occurred.

## 7. Sample chapter — Human oversight for AI-assisted travel decisions

### Requirement

Where the AI Act requires human oversight, the organization must assign oversight to natural persons who have the competence, authority, training, information, and practical ability to understand the system, recognise limitations, avoid over-reliance, intervene, override, or stop its operation as appropriate.

### Plain-language explanation

Putting a person “in the loop” is not enough. The reviewer must understand what the system can and cannot do, have enough time and information to assess the output, and possess real authority to change the result.

### Travel-agency example

GlobalWay Travel Services uses AI to flag unusual corporate-travel bookings for possible fraud. The system may identify a booking as high risk, but it may not automatically cancel the traveller’s itinerary. A trained fraud analyst reviews the evidence, checks for legitimate explanations, contacts the traveller when appropriate, and records the final decision.

### What the traveller may be concerned about

> “Will my trip be cancelled by a machine without anyone listening to me?”

### Control activity

GlobalWay shall:

1. classify the use case and document whether the system is high risk or otherwise regulated;
2. assign named oversight roles;
3. define AI authority limits;
4. require human approval before cancellation, denial, employee action, or another consequential outcome;
5. provide reviewers with system limitations, confidence indicators, relevant input data, and escalation procedures;
6. require reviewers to record approve, correct, reject, pause, or escalate decisions;
7. monitor automation bias, override rates, false positives, complaints, and harm indicators;
8. suspend the system when oversight cannot be exercised effectively.

### Evidence

- approved AI use-case record;
- human-oversight plan;
- role and authority matrix;
- training records;
- reviewer instructions;
- decision logs and override records;
- traveller notices and complaint routes;
- monitoring reports;
- incident and corrective-action records.

### Audit test

Select a sample of AI-flagged bookings and verify that:

1. a trained reviewer assessed each consequential case;
2. the reviewer had authority to change the AI recommendation;
3. the final action and rationale were recorded;
4. escalation rules were followed;
5. affected travellers received required information;
6. complaints or corrections were tracked;
7. recurring false positives produced corrective action.

## 9. Visual-design standard

Every figure must:

- explain a real process or human concern;
- use a clear reading order;
- identify the human decision point;
- show escalation or correction where relevant;
- use accessible contrast and redundant cues beyond colour;
- use concise, plain-language labels;
- include a numbered caption and meaningful alt text;
- be explained in the surrounding text;
- avoid implying that AI is autonomous, infallible, or accountable;
- avoid decorative robots, abstract circuitry, or ornamental dashboards unless they add instructional meaning.
