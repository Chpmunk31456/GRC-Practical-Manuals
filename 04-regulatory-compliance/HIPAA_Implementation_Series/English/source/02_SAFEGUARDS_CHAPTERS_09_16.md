# Manual 06 — HIPAA Implementation and Audit
## Controlled English Source — Chapters 09–16

> Controlled English implementation guidance. Current HIPAA requirements are kept separate from proposed Security Rule changes. Proposed changes may inform readiness planning only and must not be represented as current law.

## Chapter 09 — Workforce authorization and access lifecycle

Workforce access should be granted according to role, job need, authorization, and minimum-necessary principles where applicable. Joiner, mover, and leaver processes should define approval, provisioning, review, modification, suspension, and termination responsibilities.

Evidence should include access requests, approvals, role definitions, periodic reviews, privileged-access records, termination timing, and exception handling. Shared or orphaned accounts should be identified and remediated.

## Chapter 10 — Identity, authentication, and privileged access

Systems containing ePHI should use identity and authentication controls appropriate to risk and technology. Privileged access should be limited, separately governed, monitored, and reviewed.

Administrative and service-account use should be traceable where feasible. Credentials, tokens, keys, and secrets should be protected throughout their lifecycle, and emergency-access procedures should be controlled and auditable.

## Chapter 11 — Technical access and transmission controls

Technical safeguards should control access to ePHI and protect information during transmission according to the applicable environment and risk. Implementation records should identify access-control mechanisms, unique-user identification, authentication, session behavior, remote-access controls, encryption or equivalent protections where used, and network or application transmission paths.

Control descriptions should reflect actual deployed configurations rather than policy statements alone.

## Chapter 12 — Logging, monitoring, and audit controls

Organizations should define security-relevant logging and review sufficient to support accountability, detection, investigation, and evidence. Relevant records may include authentication events, privilege changes, access to sensitive data, administrative actions, security alerts, configuration changes, and system events.

Logging scope, retention, access protection, review frequency, alert ownership, escalation, and evidence preservation should be documented. Missing logs or unreviewed critical alerts should be treated as risk rather than assumed control coverage.

## Chapter 13 — Endpoint, server, and infrastructure protection

Endpoints, servers, virtual infrastructure, cloud services, and other platforms that create, receive, maintain, or transmit ePHI should be managed through risk-based configuration, vulnerability, patching, malware-defense, access, backup, and monitoring practices appropriate to the environment.

Evidence should distinguish implemented safeguards from planned safeguards and record exceptions, unsupported systems, compensating controls, and remediation ownership.

## Chapter 14 — Physical safeguards

Physical safeguards should address facilities, workstations, devices, media, and environmental conditions that can affect the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of ePHI. Access to sensitive locations and equipment should be limited and managed according to business and security needs.

Device and media controls should cover movement, reuse, disposal, sanitization, inventory, loss, and transfer where applicable. Physical safeguards should be coordinated with workforce, technical, and contingency controls rather than reviewed in isolation.

## Chapter 15 — Backup, contingency, and availability

Contingency planning should identify critical systems and data, backup requirements, restoration methods, emergency operating needs, communications, dependencies, and testing responsibilities. Recovery objectives should reflect business and patient-care consequences where relevant.

Backups should be protected, monitored, and periodically tested for recoverability. A backup process should not be credited as effective solely because jobs report success; restoration evidence should be retained for representative tests.

## Chapter 16 — Security awareness and workforce training

Workforce members should receive security and privacy training appropriate to their responsibilities, access, risk, and technology use. Training should address expected behavior, incident reporting, credential protection, phishing or social-engineering risk, handling of PHI/ePHI, remote work, and role-specific obligations where relevant.

Evidence should show assignment, completion, exceptions, overdue items, supplemental training after incidents or material changes, and accountability for persistent non-compliance.
