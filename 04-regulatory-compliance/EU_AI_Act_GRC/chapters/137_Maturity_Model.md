# Chapter 137 — Maturity Model

## Purpose

This chapter provides a practical maturity model for assessing how consistently, effectively, and sustainably an organization governs AI systems and meets its EU AI Act-related obligations.

AI may support human decision-making, but it must not remove human responsibility, judgment, or accountability.

## Requirement

Organizations should periodically assess the maturity of their AI governance capabilities using documented criteria, reliable evidence, accountable ownership, and risk-based improvement plans. Maturity should reflect actual operating practice rather than policy statements alone.

## Plain-language explanation

A maturity model helps management understand whether AI governance is informal, repeatable, controlled, measured, or continuously improving. It should not be used to create an artificial score or imply compliance merely because a numerical level has been assigned.

## Maturity levels

### Level 1 — Initial

Characteristics:

- AI use is decentralized or poorly understood;
- inventories are incomplete;
- accountability is unclear;
- controls are largely reactive;
- documentation is inconsistent;
- legal classification is performed only after problems arise;
- evidence depends on individuals;
- incidents and complaints are handled informally.

### Level 2 — Developing

Characteristics:

- governance roles are emerging;
- priority systems are inventoried;
- basic policies and intake processes exist;
- high-risk and prohibited-practice screening has begun;
- controls are defined for selected systems;
- training is available but not fully role-based;
- remediation is tracked inconsistently;
- assurance remains limited.

### Level 3 — Defined

Characteristics:

- enterprise policy, roles, and decision rights are documented;
- material systems are inventoried and classified;
- lifecycle gates are standardized;
- control ownership, frequency, and evidence requirements are defined;
- vendor, privacy, security, and human-oversight controls are integrated;
- findings and exceptions are governed;
- management reporting is established;
- internal assurance is planned.

### Level 4 — Managed

Characteristics:

- controls operate consistently across business units and jurisdictions;
- monitoring, metrics, and thresholds are in use;
- system and vendor changes trigger reassessment;
- technical validation and operating-effectiveness testing are routine;
- evidence is version matched and retrievable;
- executives and the board receive reliable risk reporting;
- regulatory, incident, and continuity readiness are exercised;
- repeat findings are actively reduced.

### Level 5 — Optimizing

Characteristics:

- governance is embedded into strategy, procurement, development, and operations;
- reliable automation supports inventory, evidence, and monitoring;
- lessons from incidents, audits, complaints, and regulatory developments drive change;
- cross-system and concentration risks are analyzed;
- controls are simplified or strengthened based on evidence;
- assurance is continuous and risk-responsive;
- the organization can adapt rapidly without losing accountability or traceability.

## Assessment domains

Assess maturity across:

- governance and board oversight;
- AI inventory and ownership;
- legal applicability and role classification;
- prohibited-practice and high-risk screening;
- risk and fundamental-rights management;
- data governance and privacy;
- technical documentation and validation;
- human oversight and transparency;
- cybersecurity and resilience;
- third-party and supply-chain governance;
- incident, complaint, and corrective-action management;
- control design, evidence, and testing;
- regulatory readiness;
- AI literacy and competence;
- multijurisdictional deployment;
- continuous monitoring and improvement.

## Scoring principles

Use conservative scoring:

- score each domain separately;
- require objective evidence;
- use the lowest reliably demonstrated level when practices vary materially;
- distinguish design maturity from operating maturity;
- record exceptions, limitations, and confidence;
- avoid averaging away critical weaknesses;
- do not allow strong documentation to compensate for ineffective operation;
- do not treat maturity scores as legal conclusions.

## Evidence expectations

Evidence may include:

- approved policies and charters;
- inventories and classification records;
- control registers and ownership assignments;
- completed assessments and approvals;
- training and competence records;
- testing, monitoring, and incident data;
- vendor and contractual records;
- audit and assurance reports;
- findings, exceptions, and remediation;
- executive and board materials;
- regulatory correspondence and exercise results.

## Gap analysis and target state

For each domain:

1. document the current level;
2. identify evidence and confidence;
3. define the target level based on risk and obligations;
4. record the gap;
5. assign accountable actions, resources, and deadlines;
6. define measurable completion criteria;
7. reassess after implementation.

Not every domain needs Level 5. The target should be proportionate to legal obligations, system risk, scale, complexity, and organizational context.

## GlobalWay Travel Services example

GlobalWay assesses its programme as Level 3 overall. Governance, inventory, and lifecycle approvals are defined, but monitoring and assurance vary across regions. Technical validation is Level 4 for recruitment and fraud systems, while vendor evidence and localized transparency remain Level 2 in several countries.

Management sets a Level 4 target for high-risk, privacy, vendor, and regulatory-readiness domains and funds a 12-month improvement plan. The board receives both the maturity ratings and the evidence limitations behind them.

## Control activities

- Approve a maturity framework and scoring criteria.
- Assess each domain using objective evidence.
- Separate design and operating maturity.
- Apply conservative scoring and confidence ratings.
- Define risk-based target levels.
- Link gaps to funded remediation plans.
- Report critical domain weaknesses separately from averages.
- Reassess periodically and after material change.

## Evidence

- maturity methodology;
- domain criteria;
- assessor competence and independence records;
- evidence index;
- scoring worksheets;
- confidence and limitation records;
- gap analysis;
- target-state approvals;
- improvement roadmap;
- executive and board reports;
- reassessment results.

## Audit tests

1. Verify maturity criteria are defined and consistently applied.
2. Trace selected scores to objective evidence.
3. Confirm design and operating maturity are distinguished.
4. Review whether critical weaknesses were obscured by averaging.
5. Assess whether target levels are proportionate to risk and obligations.
6. Trace maturity gaps to accountable, funded actions.
7. Confirm reassessment occurred after remediation or material change.

## Metrics

- maturity level by domain;
- domains below target;
- high-risk domains at Level 3 or above;
- maturity actions overdue;
- maturity scores with low evidence confidence;
- repeat weaknesses;
- business-unit and jurisdiction variation;
- time required to advance priority domains;
- maturity improvements validated independently.

## Management checklist

- Are maturity ratings supported by actual evidence?
- Are design and operating effectiveness assessed separately?
- Are critical weaknesses visible despite overall scores?
- Are target levels tied to risk and legal obligations?
- Are improvement actions funded and assigned?
- Does reassessment verify that maturity actually improved?
