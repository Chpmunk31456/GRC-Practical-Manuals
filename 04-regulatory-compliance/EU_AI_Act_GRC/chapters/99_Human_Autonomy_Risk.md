# Chapter 99 — Human-Autonomy Risk

## Purpose

This chapter defines a practical method for assessing and controlling the risk that an AI system weakens meaningful human choice, judgment, control, or accountability.

AI may support human decision-making, but it must not remove human responsibility, judgment, or accountability.

## Requirement

Organizations should assess whether people retain meaningful control over consequential AI-assisted processes. The assessment should consider affected persons, users, operators, reviewers, managers, and administrators, and should examine whether system design, incentives, workload, interface choices, or organizational culture create undue reliance, manipulation, coercion, or loss of practical choice.

## Plain-language explanation

A human being in the process does not automatically mean there is meaningful human oversight. A reviewer may technically be able to override an AI recommendation but lack the time, information, authority, training, or confidence to do so. Human-autonomy risk exists when people become rubber stamps, when affected persons cannot make an informed choice, or when AI output is presented in a way that pressures or steers behavior unfairly.

## Autonomy dimensions

Assess:

1. **Awareness** — does the person know AI is involved?
2. **Understanding** — can the person understand the role and limitations of the system?
3. **Choice** — can the person decline, correct, or seek an alternative where appropriate?
4. **Control** — can an authorized human intervene, override, pause, or stop the system?
5. **Authority** — does the reviewer have real decision rights?
6. **Capacity** — does the reviewer have enough time, competence, and information?
7. **Freedom from manipulation** — are interface, content, and incentives designed without improper pressure or exploitation?
8. **Accountability** — is a human owner responsible for the outcome?

## Common risk scenarios

- operators approve recommendations without independent review;
- confidence scores or rankings create false authority;
- human overrides are discouraged or penalized;
- users cannot reach a human representative;
- vulnerable persons are influenced through personalized persuasion;
- choices are hidden, preselected, or framed unfairly;
- workers are evaluated by systems they cannot challenge;
- the reviewer lacks relevant context;
- high workload makes meaningful review impossible;
- automated escalation replaces professional judgment;
- a nominal kill switch is inaccessible or ineffective;
- management treats AI output as objective fact.

## Assessment factors

Rate risk using factors such as:

- consequence and reversibility;
- vulnerability of affected persons;
- degree of automation;
- time available for review;
- clarity of system limitations;
- reviewer competence;
- authority to override;
- quality of alternatives;
- ease of contesting or appealing;
- evidence of automation bias;
- organizational incentives;
- interface design and persuasive techniques;
- availability of manual fallback.

## Meaningful human oversight criteria

Oversight is meaningful when the reviewer:

- understands the system’s intended purpose;
- receives relevant context, uncertainty, and limitations;
- can identify out-of-scope or anomalous cases;
- has enough time to review;
- has authority to reject, change, or stop the output;
- is not punished for appropriate overrides;
- records reasons for consequential decisions;
- can escalate uncertainty;
- is periodically assessed for automation bias and control effectiveness.

## Control expectations

Controls may include:

- defined human decision points;
- role-based authority and segregation of duties;
- mandatory review for high-consequence actions;
- override, pause, stop, and rollback functions;
- human escalation visible to users;
- workload and staffing standards;
- decision-support interfaces that show uncertainty and alternatives;
- training on automation bias and manipulation;
- override monitoring without discouraging legitimate use;
- contest and appeal mechanisms;
- manual fallback procedures;
- governance review of persuasive or personalized design.

## GlobalWay Travel Services example

GlobalWay introduces an AI tool that proposes emergency itinerary changes. Consultants begin accepting recommendations quickly during major disruptions because the interface places the top recommendation in a prominent green box while alternatives are hidden.

GlobalWay redesigns the interface to show key constraints, uncertainty, and viable alternatives. It requires human approval for changes involving accessibility needs, unaccompanied minors, major fare differences, or missed connections. Consultants are trained to challenge recommendations, and management monitors whether staffing levels allow meaningful review during peak events.

## Control activities

- Define where human judgment is mandatory.
- Test whether reviewers have time, information, authority, and competence.
- Provide practical intervention and fallback mechanisms.
- Monitor automation bias, override patterns, and workload.
- Protect affected persons from manipulative design.
- Provide challenge, correction, and appeal routes.
- Assign accountable human ownership for outcomes.

## Evidence

- human-oversight plan;
- role and authority matrix;
- interface and workflow designs;
- override and escalation logs;
- workload and staffing analysis;
- training and competence records;
- usability and human-factors testing;
- appeal and complaint records;
- manual fallback tests;
- management review minutes;
- risk-acceptance decisions.

## Audit tests

1. Identify consequential AI-assisted decisions and verify required human decision points.
2. Observe or simulate whether reviewers can understand, challenge, override, and stop the system.
3. Review workload, staffing, and time available for meaningful review.
4. Analyze override rates and investigate unusually low or high patterns.
5. Confirm users and affected persons can reach a human or contest outcomes where required.
6. Test manual fallback and emergency-stop arrangements.
7. Review whether incentives or interface design encourage blind reliance.

## Metrics

- consequential decisions requiring human review;
- override and rejection rates;
- review time by case type;
- escalation and appeal volumes;
- successful corrections;
- reviewer training completion;
- workload threshold breaches;
- fallback tests completed;
- incidents involving automation bias;
- systems without effective stop or override capability.

## Management checklist

- Is human oversight real rather than nominal?
- Do reviewers have sufficient time and authority?
- Can people challenge, correct, or appeal outcomes?
- Are vulnerable persons protected from manipulation?
- Are overrides supported rather than discouraged?
- Can the organization safely revert to manual operation?
- Is a human owner accountable for the outcome?
