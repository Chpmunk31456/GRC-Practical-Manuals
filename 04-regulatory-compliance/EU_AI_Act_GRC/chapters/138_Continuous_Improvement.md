# Chapter 138 — Continuous Improvement

## Purpose

This chapter explains how organizations should continuously improve AI governance, controls, technical safeguards, assurance, evidence, and regulatory readiness after the initial compliance programme has been implemented.

AI may support human decision-making, but it must not remove human responsibility, judgment, or accountability.

## Requirement

Organizations should maintain a documented and evidence-based continuous-improvement process that responds to regulatory developments, incidents, complaints, audit findings, control failures, model and vendor changes, performance trends, emerging risks, and lessons learned.

## Plain-language explanation

AI governance is not finished when policies are approved or a readiness project closes. Systems, models, vendors, laws, risks, and business uses change. A programme that is not reviewed and improved will gradually become inaccurate, ineffective, or disconnected from actual operations.

## Improvement inputs

Use inputs from:

- legal and regulatory developments;
- official guidance and standards;
- system and model changes;
- new use cases and jurisdictions;
- monitoring, drift, and performance data;
- incidents and near misses;
- complaints, appeals, and human overrides;
- audit, validation, and conformity findings;
- control-testing deficiencies;
- vendor changes and supply-chain events;
- cybersecurity and privacy intelligence;
- employee and user feedback;
- accessibility and inclusion testing;
- board and executive challenge;
- benchmarking and maturity assessments.

## Improvement cycle

Apply a controlled cycle:

1. identify the issue, opportunity, or change;
2. assess legal, safety, fundamental-rights, operational, and business impact;
3. prioritize based on risk and urgency;
4. assign accountable ownership and resources;
5. design corrective or preventive action;
6. test the proposed change;
7. approve and implement it through change control;
8. validate operating effectiveness;
9. update documentation, training, and evidence;
10. monitor outcomes and capture lessons learned.

## Prioritization

Prioritize improvements based on:

- actual or potential harm;
- prohibited-practice exposure;
- high-risk or GPAI obligations;
- regulatory deadline or commitment;
- control failure severity;
- affected population and scale;
- repeat incidents or findings;
- vendor concentration or dependency;
- cybersecurity and resilience impact;
- evidence weakness;
- implementation complexity and dependency;
- opportunity to prevent recurrence across multiple systems.

## Corrective and preventive action

Distinguish:

- **correction:** immediate action addressing a specific observed problem;
- **corrective action:** action removing the cause of an actual deficiency;
- **preventive action:** action reducing the likelihood of a foreseeable deficiency;
- **systemic improvement:** action addressing common causes across systems, vendors, entities, or control domains.

Do not close an item merely because a document was updated. Confirm that the change operates effectively in practice.

## Regulatory and legal change management

Maintain a process to:

- identify relevant legal and regulatory developments;
- assess applicability and effective dates;
- identify affected systems, roles, entities, and controls;
- update obligation and article-to-control mappings;
- revise policies, contracts, notices, assessments, and evidence requirements;
- communicate changes to accountable personnel;
- test implementation before the deadline;
- retain the legal analysis and implementation evidence.

## Technical and vendor change management

Continuous improvement should also respond to:

- model releases and deprecations;
- changed prompts, tools, or agent capabilities;
- new training or retrieval data;
- performance or subgroup drift;
- new vulnerabilities and attack methods;
- vendor terms, locations, subprocessors, or service levels;
- logging or evidence limitations;
- infrastructure and architecture changes;
- emerging open-source dependencies;
- changes affecting intended purpose or legal role.

## Learning from incidents and complaints

For material events:

- reconstruct the timeline;
- preserve evidence;
- identify technical, process, governance, and human causes;
- assess whether similar systems share the same weakness;
- implement immediate containment;
- assign corrective and preventive actions;
- validate closure independently;
- update scenarios, training, controls, and monitoring;
- report material lessons to executives and the board.

## Control optimization

Periodically review controls to determine whether they are:

- legally aligned;
- clearly owned;
- proportionate to risk;
- consistently performed;
- supported by reliable evidence;
- duplicative or unnecessarily burdensome;
- capable of detecting meaningful failure;
- integrated into business workflows;
- resilient to system and vendor change;
- suitable for automation without reducing accountability.

Control simplification is appropriate only when it preserves or improves protection, traceability, and legal compliance.

## GlobalWay Travel Services example

GlobalWay’s quarterly review identifies repeated failures to update localized transparency notices after vendor model changes. The company determines that the issue is not isolated to one product; the change-management process does not automatically notify regional legal and content owners.

GlobalWay adds a mandatory jurisdiction-impact step to vendor-change intake, integrates notice owners into the approval workflow, updates training, and tests the control across three systems. Internal audit validates the change before the corrective action is closed.

## Control activities

- Maintain an approved continuous-improvement process.
- Collect improvement inputs from legal, technical, operational, and assurance sources.
- Prioritize based on risk, harm, and deadlines.
- Distinguish correction, corrective action, preventive action, and systemic improvement.
- Apply controlled testing and approval before implementation.
- Validate effectiveness before closure.
- Update policies, controls, training, documentation, and evidence.
- Report material trends and systemic issues to executives and the board.

## Evidence

- legal and regulatory change register;
- improvement backlog;
- risk and priority assessments;
- root-cause analyses;
- corrective and preventive action plans;
- change approvals;
- test and validation results;
- updated control and policy records;
- training and communication evidence;
- closure validation;
- trend and lessons-learned reports;
- executive and board reporting.

## Audit tests

1. Review whether relevant legal, technical, vendor, and incident inputs enter the improvement process.
2. Trace selected improvements from identification through prioritization, implementation, and validation.
3. Confirm root-cause analysis addresses systemic causes where appropriate.
4. Verify corrective actions were not closed solely on documentary evidence.
5. Assess whether changes triggered updates to controls, training, and evidence requirements.
6. Review repeat findings for ineffective remediation.
7. Confirm material lessons and residual risk were reported to management and the board.

## Metrics

- improvement actions opened and closed;
- overdue corrective and preventive actions;
- repeat findings and incidents;
- average time from issue identification to containment;
- average time from remediation to validated closure;
- legal changes implemented before deadline;
- systemic issues identified across multiple systems;
- controls simplified or automated with validated effectiveness;
- improvements producing measurable risk reduction;
- residual risks accepted after unsuccessful remediation.

## Management checklist

- Are we learning from incidents, complaints, audits, and monitoring?
- Are legal and vendor changes translated into timely control updates?
- Are root causes addressed rather than symptoms alone?
- Is effectiveness tested before closure?
- Are repeat problems visible and escalated?
- Does continuous improvement produce measurable risk reduction?
