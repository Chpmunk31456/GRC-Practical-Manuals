# Chapter 125 — Administrative Fines and Exposure

## Purpose

This chapter explains how organizations should identify, assess, govern, and report potential administrative-fine exposure and related legal, financial, operational, and reputational consequences arising from AI noncompliance.

AI may support human decision-making, but it must not remove human responsibility, judgment, or accountability.

## Requirement

Organizations should maintain a documented process for evaluating potential enforcement exposure, including the nature of the suspected violation, the organization’s role, affected jurisdictions, duration, scale, intent or negligence, cooperation, remediation, prior history, and the reliability of available evidence.

## Plain-language explanation

Administrative fines are only one part of enforcement exposure. A serious AI compliance failure may also lead to system suspension, market withdrawal, corrective action, litigation, customer compensation, contractual claims, executive scrutiny, and loss of trust. Exposure should therefore be assessed as a coordinated enterprise risk rather than as a single legal estimate.

## Exposure categories

Assess at minimum:

- prohibited-practice violations;
- failure to meet high-risk-system obligations;
- provider, deployer, importer, distributor, authorized-representative, or manufacturer failures;
- GPAI and systemic-risk failures;
- transparency violations;
- inaccurate, incomplete, or misleading information supplied to authorities;
- failure to cooperate with supervisory or market-surveillance authorities;
- documentation, logging, registration, or evidence gaps;
- failure to report serious incidents or corrective actions where required;
- continued operation after known material noncompliance.

## Exposure assessment factors

Document:

- applicable legal provisions and jurisdiction;
- organization role and responsibility;
- systems, models, products, and services involved;
- affected people and rights;
- duration and geographic reach;
- number and severity of affected decisions or transactions;
- whether conduct was intentional, negligent, repeated, or concealed;
- preventive controls and their effectiveness;
- speed and quality of detection, escalation, containment, and remediation;
- cooperation with authorities;
- prior violations or findings;
- direct and indirect financial effects;
- uncertainty and legal assumptions.

## Financial impact model

A practical exposure model may include:

- potential administrative fines;
- legal-defense and investigation costs;
- forensic, validation, and remediation costs;
- customer compensation and refunds;
- system suspension, withdrawal, or replacement costs;
- vendor exit and reimplementation costs;
- operational disruption and lost revenue;
- contractual penalties and service credits;
- insurance deductibles or coverage limitations;
- communications and trust-restoration costs.

Use ranges and scenarios where exact amounts cannot be determined. Clearly distinguish legal maximums, management estimates, accounting provisions, and realized costs.

## Governance and privilege

Legal counsel should direct legal interpretation, privilege decisions, regulator strategy, and litigation risk. Finance should assess accounting and disclosure implications. Risk, compliance, security, privacy, communications, and business leaders should contribute evidence and operational analysis.

Do not use privilege labels to conceal ordinary compliance evidence or obstruct lawful oversight.

## GlobalWay Travel Services example

GlobalWay discovers that a recruitment-screening system was deployed in several EU entities without complete classification, human-oversight evidence, and vendor-change documentation. Legal counsel assesses potential regulatory exposure, while finance estimates remediation, revalidation, applicant-review, and service-replacement costs.

GlobalWay suspends automated recommendations, preserves evidence, informs executive leadership, conducts an affected-person review, and prepares a regulator-response package. The exposure assessment is updated as facts, legal analysis, and remediation evidence develop.

## Control activities

- Maintain an AI enforcement-exposure assessment procedure.
- Identify applicable roles, obligations, and jurisdictions.
- Escalate suspected material violations promptly.
- Preserve evidence and reconcile deployed versions.
- Estimate direct and indirect financial consequences.
- Coordinate legal, finance, compliance, risk, and communications.
- Update exposure assessments as facts and remediation evolve.
- Report material exposure to executives, the board, auditors, and insurers as appropriate.

## Evidence

- legal and regulatory assessments;
- incident and investigation records;
- exposure models and assumptions;
- accounting and insurance analyses;
- system suspension or restriction approvals;
- regulator correspondence;
- remediation plans and completion evidence;
- executive and board reporting;
- customer-impact and compensation analyses;
- lessons-learned reviews.

## Audit tests

1. Select material AI compliance incidents and verify exposure was assessed across legal, financial, operational, and reputational dimensions.
2. Confirm applicable roles, obligations, and jurisdictions were documented.
3. Review whether estimates distinguish maximum exposure, likely range, and realized cost.
4. Verify evidence preservation, executive escalation, and legal review occurred promptly.
5. Trace remediation actions to updated exposure assessments.
6. Confirm material matters were reported to appropriate governance bodies.
7. Review whether repeat issues influenced risk ratings and control changes.

## Metrics

- open material AI enforcement exposures;
- estimated and realized AI-related losses;
- time from detection to legal and executive escalation;
- systems suspended or restricted;
- repeat or prolonged noncompliance;
- uninsured exposure;
- overdue remediation linked to enforcement risk;
- regulatory commitments outstanding;
- customer-remediation costs.

## Management checklist

- What legal provisions and organizational roles are implicated?
- How many people, systems, entities, and jurisdictions are affected?
- What are the plausible direct and indirect financial consequences?
- Have we preserved evidence and stopped continuing harm?
- Are legal, finance, compliance, and communications aligned?
- Has the board received a clear, evidence-based assessment?
