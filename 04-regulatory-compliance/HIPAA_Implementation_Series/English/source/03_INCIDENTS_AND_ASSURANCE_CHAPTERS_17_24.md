# Manual 06 — HIPAA Implementation and Audit
## Controlled English Source — Chapters 17–24

> Controlled educational implementation guidance. This material supports evidence-based review but does not provide legal advice, establish HIPAA compliance, or determine whether an incident is reportable.

## Chapter 17 — Security incident response

HIPAA-related security events should enter a defined incident process with clear roles for detection, triage, containment, evidence preservation, technical response, privacy/compliance review, legal escalation where appropriate, recovery, and lessons learned.

Incident records should identify affected systems and information, timing, actions taken, decision owners, communications, unresolved questions, and follow-up remediation. Significant incidents should trigger reassessment of relevant risk analyses and safeguards.

## Chapter 18 — Breach assessment workflow

Potential unauthorized acquisition, access, use, or disclosure of PHI should be routed to the organization’s controlled breach-assessment process. The record should preserve facts, evidence, scope, affected information, involved persons or entities, mitigation, decision rationale, reviewers, and applicable notification analysis.

Repository or automated QA must not decide whether an event is a legally reportable breach. That determination requires qualified human review based on the applicable facts and law.

## Chapter 19 — Business associates and subcontractors

Business-associate governance should identify relationships involving PHI/ePHI, required agreements, permitted uses and disclosures, security expectations, incident and breach-notification duties, subcontractor dependencies, evidence requirements, and termination responsibilities.

A signed agreement is necessary evidence where applicable but is not proof of effective safeguards. Due diligence, ongoing monitoring, issue management, and access/data-return or destruction controls should be proportionate to risk.

## Chapter 20 — Vendor and cloud-service oversight

Cloud, SaaS, managed service, hosting, analytics, support, and other vendors handling ePHI should be assessed for architecture, access, security controls, resilience, incident response, subcontractors, evidence, and contractual obligations appropriate to the service.

Vendor changes that materially alter hosting, subprocessors, security architecture, data location, access, or control ownership should trigger reassessment rather than being silently accepted.

## Chapter 21 — Documentation and retention

HIPAA implementation evidence should be retained in a controlled manner consistent with applicable documentation requirements and organizational policy. Records should be dated, versioned where appropriate, attributable to an owner or reviewer, and protected against unauthorized alteration.

Useful evidence may include policies, procedures, risk analyses, approvals, training records, access reviews, technical configurations, logs, BAAs, incident records, breach assessments, remediation records, and evaluation results.

## Chapter 22 — Evaluation and control testing

Periodic technical and non-technical evaluation should determine whether implemented safeguards and procedures remain aligned with the organization’s environment, risks, and applicable HIPAA requirements.

Testing should use defined criteria, scope, samples where appropriate, expected results, actual results, limitations, findings, and reviewer conclusions. Evidence should distinguish control design from operating effectiveness.

## Chapter 23 — Findings, corrective action, and validation

Findings should describe the criteria or expected condition, observed condition, evidence, risk implication, severity or priority, owner, corrective action, target date, and escalation path.

Closure requires sufficient validation that the corrective action addresses the underlying issue. Administrative completion without evidence should not be treated as remediation.

## Chapter 24 — Exceptions and residual risk

Exceptions should document the unmet requirement or control expectation, business rationale, scope, compensating safeguards, residual risk, accountable owner, approver, expiration date, and monitoring requirement.

Expired or repeatedly extended exceptions should be reassessed. Where risk exceeds approved tolerance, escalation should be visible and decision authority explicit.
