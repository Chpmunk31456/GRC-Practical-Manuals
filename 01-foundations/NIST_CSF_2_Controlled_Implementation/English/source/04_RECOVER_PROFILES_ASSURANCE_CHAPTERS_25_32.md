# Manual 09 — NIST CSF 2.0 Controlled Implementation
## Controlled English Source — Chapters 25–32

> Controlled implementation guidance for RECOVER, Profiles, Tiers, informative references, assurance, and continuous improvement.

## Chapter 25 — Recovery planning and prioritization

Define recovery priorities from mission and business impact, critical-service dependencies, risk, safety, data integrity, contractual needs, and stakeholder expectations. Establish recovery roles, communication routes, dependencies, restoration criteria, and fallback options.

Plans should identify assumptions and dependencies that can fail during a real incident.

## Chapter 26 — Recovery execution and validation

Restore affected capabilities using controlled priorities and evidence-based decisions. Validate service functionality, security, data integrity, access, monitoring, and unresolved risk before declaring recovery complete.

Recovery records should distinguish temporary restoration, permanent remediation, accepted residual risk, and follow-up work.

## Chapter 27 — Recovery communication and improvement

Coordinate recovery status and expectations with leadership, business owners, technical teams, suppliers, customers, and other stakeholders as appropriate. Capture lessons and feed them into governance, risk assessment, architecture, response, and resilience planning.

Recurring recovery weaknesses should become visible improvement items rather than repeatedly accepted operational surprises.

## Chapter 28 — Organizational Profiles

Use Current and Target Profiles to describe selected CSF outcomes in organizational context, identify gaps, prioritize improvement, and communicate risk decisions. Profile scope, rationale, assumptions, owners, evidence, and review dates should be controlled.

Profiles are decision tools, not certificates or universal maturity scores.

## Chapter 29 — Community Profiles and shared requirements

Where sector, community, ecosystem, or mission-specific Profiles are used, map them transparently to organizational context. Record where community expectations apply, where organization-specific tailoring is necessary, and how conflicts or gaps are resolved.

External Profile use should not erase local risk ownership or create unsupported equivalence claims.

## Chapter 30 — Implementation Tiers and enterprise integration

Use CSF Tiers to characterize cybersecurity risk-governance and management practices in context. Tier discussions should support decisions about desired characteristics and investment without treating a Tier as a certification level or an automatic target for every organization.

Integrate cybersecurity risk with enterprise risk and workforce planning where useful, preserving accountable ownership and decision evidence.

## Chapter 31 — Informative references, crosswalks, and assurance

Use informative references to connect CSF outcomes with other standards, controls, practices, or requirements. Maintain source identity, version, mapping rationale, directionality, limitations, provenance, and validation.

Machine-readable or AI-assisted mappings require continuous evaluation and human review. A mapping must not be treated as proof that two requirements are equivalent or that an outcome is achieved.

Assurance should distinguish design, implementation, operation, and evidence quality. Formal assurance opinions require competent independent judgment and sufficient appropriate evidence.

## Chapter 32 — Release, maintenance, and continuous improvement

Manual 09 closes only when source verification, the complete controlled English master, technical/editorial/security review, localization and human semantic review, accessibility, publication QA, repository/workflow security review, provenance, changed-scope reconciliation, and Final Human Release Approval are complete for the same exact candidate.

After release, changes to NIST CSF resources, material business or threat changes, incidents, audit findings, failed tests, supplier changes, or evidence defects trigger impact analysis and, where material, a new controlled review cycle. Completion establishes a reviewed baseline; it does not guarantee future cybersecurity effectiveness.
