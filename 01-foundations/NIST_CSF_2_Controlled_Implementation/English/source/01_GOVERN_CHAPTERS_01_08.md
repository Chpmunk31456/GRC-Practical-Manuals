# Manual 09 — NIST CSF 2.0 Controlled Implementation
## Controlled English Source — Chapters 01–08

> Practical implementation guidance aligned to NIST CSF 2.0. CSF outcomes are not prescriptive controls, certification requirements, or guarantees of cybersecurity effectiveness.

## Chapter 01 — Organizational context and mission dependency

Define the mission, business objectives, critical services, stakeholders, legal and contractual context, technology environment, and external dependencies that shape cybersecurity risk. The record should make clear which outcomes matter most and why.

Implementation evidence should include scope, critical services, dependency maps, accountable owners, assumptions, and unresolved scope questions. Material business or technology change must trigger reassessment.

## Chapter 02 — Cybersecurity risk governance

Establish decision rights for cybersecurity risk, including executive accountability, escalation paths, delegated authority, oversight bodies, and relationships with enterprise risk management.

Governance evidence should show who may accept risk, who challenges decisions, how material issues reach leadership, and how cybersecurity considerations enter strategy, investment, acquisitions, outsourcing, and change decisions.

## Chapter 03 — Risk strategy and risk appetite

Translate enterprise objectives into cybersecurity risk assumptions, priorities, tolerances, response expectations, and escalation thresholds. Risk strategy should be usable in real decisions rather than limited to policy language.

Document risk appetite or equivalent decision criteria, treatment options, exception rules, review cadence, and how uncertainty is handled.

## Chapter 04 — Roles, responsibilities, and authorities

Define cybersecurity responsibilities across leadership, technology, security, legal, privacy, procurement, HR, business operations, incident management, resilience, and suppliers where applicable.

Evidence should identify accountable owners, responsible operators, consulted specialists, approval authority, segregation of duties, deputies, and gaps in required capability.

## Chapter 05 — Policy and control expectations

Policies and standards should translate risk strategy into actionable expectations while allowing context-specific implementation. Maintain ownership, approval, versioning, exceptions, review dates, and communication records.

A policy is not evidence that an outcome is achieved. Testing, operational records, technical evidence, and observed behavior are needed where relevant.

## Chapter 06 — Oversight and performance review

Leadership should review cybersecurity risk, material findings, incidents, exceptions, supplier issues, recovery readiness, and improvement progress using decision-useful information.

Metrics should connect to outcomes and risk. Activity counts alone should not be used to imply effective risk reduction.

## Chapter 07 — Cybersecurity supply-chain governance

Define how supplier, service-provider, software, cloud, data, AI, and other external dependencies enter cybersecurity governance. Apply proportional due diligence, contracting, monitoring, incident coordination, concentration analysis, and exit planning.

Supplier evidence should remain traceable to critical services and risk decisions rather than becoming a questionnaire-only compliance exercise.

## Chapter 08 — GOVERN fail-closed gate

GOVERN is incomplete when material scope is unresolved, risk authority is unclear, leadership lacks visibility into significant risk, high-risk exceptions lack authorized acceptance, or supplier dependencies are not governed proportionately.

Automated QA may validate structure and required evidence fields, but final governance adequacy requires competent human judgment.
