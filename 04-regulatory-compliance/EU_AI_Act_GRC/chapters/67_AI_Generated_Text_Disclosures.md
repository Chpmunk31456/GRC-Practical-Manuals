# Chapter 67 — AI-Generated Text Disclosures

## 67.1 Purpose

This chapter explains how an organization should govern AI-generated or AI-manipulated text that is published to inform the public on matters of public interest.

The objective is to prevent people from mistaking unreviewed AI-generated public information for accountable human-authored communication. The chapter also explains when disclosure may not be required because the content has undergone meaningful human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person accepts editorial responsibility.

> **Core principle:** A disclosure label does not replace human accountability, and human review must be real rather than ceremonial.

## 67.2 Legal Requirement

Article 50 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 requires deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate text published for the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest to disclose that the text was artificially generated or manipulated.

The disclosure obligation does not apply where both of the following conditions are met:

1. the AI-generated content has undergone a process of human review or editorial control; and
2. a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for publication of the content.

Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026, subject to applicable transitional provisions and current official guidance.

## 67.3 Plain-Language Explanation

The central question is not simply whether AI helped draft the text. The organization must determine:

- whether the text was generated or materially manipulated by AI;
- whether it is being published to inform the public;
- whether it concerns a matter of public interest;
- whether meaningful human review occurred before publication;
- whether an identifiable person or organization accepts editorial responsibility;
- whether disclosure remains necessary despite internal review.

A spell-checker, grammar assistant, or limited formatting tool does not automatically create the same risk as an AI system that drafts the substance of a public safety advisory. The organization must assess the actual role of AI in producing or altering the message.

## 67.4 What Counts as a Matter of Public Interest?

A matter of public interest may include information affecting public safety, health, transportation, government, elections, emergencies, civil rights, consumer protection, major disruptions, or other issues on which members of the public may reasonably rely.

For a travel-management company, examples may include:

- widespread airline or airport disruption;
- border closures or entry restrictions;
- public-health travel requirements;
- severe weather and evacuation information;
- security incidents affecting travelers;
- major transportation strikes;
- emergency accommodation or repatriation information;
- public statements about a significant data breach or service outage.

Routine personalized itinerary text, private customer correspondence, or ordinary commercial copy may fall outside this specific public-interest-text obligation, although other transparency, consumer-protection, privacy, advertising, or contractual duties may still apply.

## 67.5 GlobalWay Travel Services Example

A volcanic eruption closes regional airspace. GlobalWay uses a generative AI system to draft a public advisory summarizing affected airports, expected delays, rebooking options, and emergency contact information.

### Scenario A — Unreviewed AI publication

The draft is published automatically to GlobalWay’s website and social channels without human validation.

**Required response:** The text should be clearly disclosed as AI-generated or AI-manipulated. The publication control should also be treated as deficient because safety-critical public information was released without accountable review.

### Scenario B — Meaningful editorial review

A trained disruption manager verifies each operational fact against authoritative airline, airport, government, and meteorological sources; corrects the draft; removes unsupported statements; approves the final language; records the review; and GlobalWay accepts editorial responsibility.

**Potential result:** The Article 50 text-disclosure exception may apply, provided the review and editorial responsibility are genuine and demonstrable.

### Scenario C — Rubber-stamp approval

A communications employee clicks “approved” without checking sources or changing the AI draft.

**Required response:** GlobalWay should not treat this as meaningful human review. Disclosure remains appropriate, and the approval process should be remediated.

## 67.6 Decision Framework

GlobalWay shall apply the following decision sequence before publication:

1. **Was AI used to generate or materially manipulate the text?**
2. **Will the text be published or otherwise made available to the public?**
3. **Is its purpose to inform the public on a matter of public interest?**
4. **Did a qualified human conduct meaningful review or editorial control?**
5. **Is an identifiable natural or legal person accepting editorial responsibility?**
6. **Is disclosure required, prudent, or otherwise mandated?**
7. **Are evidence and publication records complete?**

When classification is uncertain, the content owner must escalate to legal or compliance before publication.

## 67.7 Meaningful Human Review Standard

Human review must be capable of changing the outcome. It should include, as appropriate:

- verifying facts against authoritative and current sources;
- checking dates, locations, names, eligibility conditions, and deadlines;
- identifying hallucinated or unsupported statements;
- evaluating safety, accessibility, bias, and vulnerable-person impacts;
- correcting misleading emphasis or omissions;
- ensuring that uncertainty is communicated honestly;
- confirming that legal and operational approvals are complete;
- recording who reviewed, changed, approved, or rejected the content.

A person who lacks time, competence, authority, information, or practical ability to challenge the output is not providing meaningful review.

## 67.8 Editorial Responsibility

GlobalWay must identify the person or legal entity responsible for the final publication.

Editorial responsibility includes:

- authority to approve, correct, delay, or reject publication;
- accountability for accuracy and appropriateness;
- responsibility for correction or withdrawal;
- ownership of complaints and affected-person communication;
- retention of the evidence supporting publication;
- escalation of material errors or incidents.

An anonymous workflow, generic mailbox, or statement that “the AI wrote it” is not adequate accountability.

## 67.9 Disclosure Design

Where disclosure is required, it must be clear, distinguishable, timely, accessible, and understandable.

Good disclosure examples include:

- “This public advisory was generated with artificial intelligence and has not been independently reviewed.”
- “This update contains AI-generated text. Contact GlobalWay for human confirmation before relying on it.”
- “AI-generated draft; operational details may change. Verified sources are linked below.”

Disclosure should be placed where a person will encounter it before or at the same time as the content. It should not be hidden in terms of service, metadata, a footer far from the text, or an ambiguous icon without explanation.

## 67.10 What AI May Do and What Remains Human

| Governance element | GlobalWay requirement |
|---|---|
| What AI may do | Draft, summarize, translate, reformat, or propose public communications |
| What remains human | Determine whether publication is appropriate and approve the final message |
| Review requirement | Verify facts, sources, uncertainty, accessibility, legal implications, and affected-person impact |
| Stop condition | Conflicting sources, missing authority, safety risk, unclear public-interest classification, or unavailable qualified reviewer |
| Escalation route | Communications lead, operations owner, legal, compliance, security, privacy, or crisis management |
| Accountable owner | Named publication owner or responsible legal entity |
| Challenge right | Members of the public may request clarification, correction, or human contact |

## 67.11 Translation and Localization

AI-generated public-interest text may be translated into multiple languages, but translation can introduce new errors or materially alter meaning.

GlobalWay shall:

- use approved terminology and official place names;
- verify safety-critical translations with qualified reviewers;
- preserve warnings, uncertainty, dates, and legal meaning;
- avoid publishing a translated version before adequate review;
- retain the source text, translation record, reviewer, and approval evidence;
- provide accessible alternatives where required.

A human review of the English master does not automatically validate every translated edition.

## 67.12 Accessibility and Vulnerable Persons

Public-interest notices should be understandable to people with different abilities, language skills, stress levels, and access conditions.

Controls should address:

- plain language;
- screen-reader compatibility;
- logical heading structure;
- sufficient contrast;
- text alternatives for visual content;
- mobile readability;
- language availability;
- non-digital contact options;
- clear human-escalation routes.

## 67.13 Publication Controls

GlobalWay shall implement:

- content classification before publication;
- system and model identification;
- mandatory review for safety-critical or high-impact public communications;
- source verification checklists;
- role-based approval authority;
- disclosure templates;
- version and change history;
- publication logs;
- correction, withdrawal, and emergency-update procedures;
- monitoring for altered or stripped disclosures downstream.

Automated publication should be disabled for high-impact public-interest text unless an approved exception and equivalent safeguards exist.

## 67.14 Stop and Escalation Conditions

Publication must stop when:

- authoritative sources conflict;
- required facts cannot be verified;
- a qualified reviewer is unavailable;
- the AI output invents or materially distorts information;
- the message could create safety, discrimination, financial, or legal harm;
- the disclosure decision is unresolved;
- required accessibility or language support is missing;
- editorial responsibility is unclear;
- a material system or prompt change has not been assessed.

## 67.15 Evidence

Retain:

- original AI-generated or manipulated text;
- prompts or instructions where lawful and appropriate;
- system, provider, model, and version information;
- source materials used for verification;
- classification decision;
- public-interest assessment;
- human-review record;
- edits and version history;
- reviewer and approver identity;
- editorial-responsibility assignment;
- disclosure wording and placement evidence;
- publication timestamp and channel;
- complaints, corrections, withdrawals, and incident records.

## 67.16 Metrics

Management should review:

- number of AI-assisted public-interest publications;
- percentage receiving meaningful human review;
- percentage requiring disclosure;
- review turnaround time;
- correction and withdrawal rates;
- factual-error rate;
- complaints or challenges;
- missing or altered disclosures;
- translation-related errors;
- publications stopped or escalated before release;
- recurring root causes.

A low correction rate is not automatically evidence of control effectiveness if monitoring is weak.

## 67.17 Audit Test

An auditor should:

1. Obtain the inventory of AI-assisted public communications.
2. Select samples across websites, social media, email, mobile applications, and crisis channels.
3. Determine whether each sampled text was AI-generated or materially manipulated.
4. Test the public-interest classification and rationale.
5. Verify whether required disclosures were clear and timely.
6. Where no disclosure was used, test both meaningful human review and editorial responsibility.
7. Inspect source verification, edits, approvals, and timestamps.
8. Test translated versions independently.
9. Review complaints, corrections, withdrawals, and incidents.
10. Confirm that recurring failures lead to control improvement.

### Audit failure examples

- an employee clicks “approved” without validating facts;
- no one can identify the accountable editor;
- the disclosure is hidden or ambiguous;
- translated versions are published without review;
- records show only the final text and not the review evidence;
- AI drafts are published automatically during emergencies;
- the organization treats all marketing and public-safety text as equivalent;
- corrections are made silently without preserving the prior version.

## 67.20 Management Review Questions

Senior management should ask:

- Which public communications are produced or materially changed by AI?
- Who determines whether a matter is of public interest?
- Can reviewers demonstrate how they validated facts?
- Who accepts editorial responsibility?
- Are disclosures visible and understandable?
- Are translated editions reviewed independently?
- What content was stopped, corrected, or withdrawn?
- Are emergency workflows more controlled or less controlled under pressure?

## 67.21 Implementation Checklist

- [ ] Inventory AI-assisted public communications.
- [ ] Define public-interest classification criteria.
- [ ] Establish meaningful human-review requirements.
- [ ] Assign editorial responsibility.
- [ ] Create approved disclosure templates.
- [ ] Disable uncontrolled automatic publication.
- [ ] Verify sources, uncertainty, and accessibility.
- [ ] Review each translated version.
- [ ] Preserve drafts, edits, approvals, and publication evidence.
- [ ] Monitor disclosures, complaints, corrections, and withdrawals.

## 67.22 Key Takeaway

The control is not satisfied merely because a human account clicked “publish.” Organizations must be able to show either a clear disclosure or a genuine process of human review and editorial responsibility.
